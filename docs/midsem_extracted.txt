Chapter 1
Introduction
Weather forecasting in polar regions presents unique scientific and operational challenges.
The Antarctic continent, characterised by extreme cold, strong katabatic winds, and limited
observational infrastructure, remains one of the most data-sparse regions on Earth. Accurate
meteorological prediction over Antarctica is critical for the safety of research personnel,
logistics planning of polar expeditions, and understanding of global climate systems.
Traditional Numerical Weather Prediction (NWP) systems such as ECMWF's Integrated
Forecasting System (IFS) rely on physics-based dynamical models that are computationally
intensive and struggle to represent fine-scale polar phenomena. In recent years, data-driven
deep learning models have emerged as powerful alternatives, demonstrating skill comparable
to or exceeding operational NWP systems at a fraction of the computational cost [14].
FourCastNet (Fourier Forecasting Neural Network), developed by Pathak et al. (2022) at
NVIDIA, represents a landmark in this direction. It employs the Adaptive Fourier Neural
Operator (AFNO) as its core computational block, enabling efficient modelling of global
atmospheric dynamics by operating in the Fourier frequency domain. FourCastNet demon-
strated competitive skill against ECMWF's IFS on global forecasting benchmarks while
being orders of magnitude faster at inference.
This project adapts the FourCastNet philosophy to a regional Antarctic context. A lightweight
AFNO-based model is trained on ERA5 reanalysis data over the Antarctic domain, with spe-
cial attention to the Maitri research station region -- India's permanent station in Antarctica.
The goal is to demonstrate the feasibility of Fourier-based deep learning forecasting at the
regional polar scale, laying the groundwork for future high-resolution deployments on HPC
infrastructure.
1.1
Objectives
The primary objectives of this project are:
1. To acquire, preprocess, and structure ERA5 reanalysis data over the Antarctic domain
for deep learning-based training.
2. To implement a lightweight AFNO-based FourCastNet architecture adapted for re-
gional polar forecasting.

Chapter 2
Literature Review
This chapter surveys the key works that motivate and contextualise the present project,
spanning data-driven weather prediction, neural operators, and Antarctic meteorological
modelling.
2.1
Data-Driven Weather Prediction
The application of deep learning to weather forecasting has a long history, but the emer-
gence of global-scale transformer-based models marks a qualitative shift. Weyn et al. (2020)
demonstrated the viability of convolutional neural networks for medium-range forecasting
on a cubed-sphere grid [19], while Rasp and Thuerey (2021) showed that purely data-driven
models could begin to approach NWP skill levels [16].
Pangu-Weather [2], developed by Huawei, employs a 3D Earth Transformer architecture
to achieve superior 10-day forecast accuracy compared to ECMWF's operational model.
GraphCast [11], developed by Google DeepMind, uses a graph neural network over a multi-
scale icosahedral mesh and achieves state-of-the-art skill on 1380 forecast targets. Aurora
[4] further pushes the boundary with a large foundation model approach.
These works
collectively establish that data-driven models can serve as viable operational forecasting
tools.
2.2
Fourier Neural Operators and FourCastNet
Neural Operators represent a paradigm for learning mappings between function spaces, en-
abling resolution-independent generalisation. The Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) proposed
by Li et al. (2021) applies spectral convolutions in the Fourier domain to learn PDE solution
operators efficiently [12]. The Adaptive Fourier Neural Operator (AFNO), introduced by
Guibas et al. (2021), extends this to vision transformer-style architectures [18] with learned
Fourier mixing [5].
FourCastNet [14] integrates AFNO blocks into an end-to-end autoregressive forecasting ar-
chitecture trained on ERA5. It demonstrated a 45├ù speedup over ECMWF IFS at inference
while matching forecast skill at 2-week lead times for many variables. Its open-source release
made regional adaptation projects such as the present one feasible.

2.3
Antarctic Meteorological Modelling
Antarctic weather forecasting has historically relied on global NWP output supplemented by
regional atmospheric models such as the Polar WRF [3, 17] and AMPS (Antarctic Mesoscale
Prediction System) [15]. These systems, while physically principled, are computationally
expensive and require regular updates to boundary conditions from global models.
The limited observational density over Antarctica -- few radiosonde stations, sparse surface
networks, and challenging satellite retrieval -- makes data assimilation difficult. Deep learn-
ing models trained on reanalysis data offer a promising alternative for regional deployment,
particularly for research stations such as Maitri and Bharati operated by NCPOR.

4.3
Adaptive Fourier Neural Operator (AFNO) Block
The AFNO block [5] is the core computational unit of FourCastNet, enabling efficient global
atmospheric interaction modelling by operating in the Fourier frequency domain.
Each
AFNO block processes a spatial feature map z ΓêêRHΓÇ▓├ùW ΓÇ▓├ùd through:
1. Fourier Transform: ╦åz = F(z)
2. Frequency-domain mixing: ╦åzΓÇ▓ = ╧â(W2 ┬╖ ╧â(W1 ┬╖ ╦åz)),
╧â = GELU
3. Inverse Fourier Transform: zΓÇ▓ = FΓêÆ1(╦åzΓÇ▓)
4. Residual connection: zΓÇ▓ΓÇ▓ = z + zΓÇ▓
The full AFNO block follows the standard pre-norm transformer structure:
zΓäô+1 = zΓäô+ MLP(LN(zΓäô+ AFNO(LN(zΓäô))))
(4.2)
where LN denotes Layer Normalisation [1], residual connections follow the design of He et
al. [7], and the MLP uses hidden dimension ratio r = 4. A total of N = 6 AFNO blocks are
stacked sequentially.
4.4
Patch Recovery
Patch recovery inverts the patch embedding using a transposed 2D convolution:
╦åx = ConvTranspose2D(z; kernel = P, stride = P, Cout = V ┬╖ O)
(4.3)
Output is reshaped to ╦åX ΓêêRB├ùO├ùV ├ùH├ùW, where B is batch size.
4.5
Dynamic Grid Support
All modules -- patch embedding, positional embedding, AFNO blocks, and patch recovery
-- are refactored to automatically infer spatial dimensions from input data. Combined with
automatic padding, this enables deployment on the Maitri regional grid and any arbitrary
ERA5 subdomain without modifying model code.

Chapter 6
Results and Discussion
6.1
Training Convergence
The model was trained for 30 epochs on the ERA5 Antarctic dataset. As seen in Fig. 7.1,
both training and validation MSE decrease sharply in the first 10 epochs, with the training
loss starting at approximately 0.22 and dropping to around 0.08 by epoch 30. The validation
loss converges to approximately 0.10 after epoch 15 and stabilises, indicating that the model
reaches a generalisation plateau around this point. The gap between training and validation
loss in later epochs suggests mild overfitting, expected given the limited 5-year dataset.
6.2
Temperature Forecast Skill
The temperature forecast scatter plot (Fig. 7.2) shows a tight alignment along the diagonal
for normalised temperature values across the range [ΓêÆ2.5, 2.0]. The scatter broadens slightly
at the extremes, corresponding to rare anomalous temperature events that are harder to
capture from the 48-hour input history. Overall, the high linear correlation indicates the
model has learned the dominant temperature climatology of the region effectively.
6.3
Spatial Forecast Quality
Fig. 7.3 presents Truth, Prediction, and Error maps for all four forecast steps (t+1 through
t+4, each 6 hours apart). Key observations:
ΓÇó The model correctly reproduces the large-scale latitudinal temperature gradient be-
tween the warmer coastal band (yellow, lower latitudes) and the colder Antarctic in-
terior (dark blue/purple, higher latitudes) at all lead times.
ΓÇó The prediction panels closely mirror the truth panels at t+1 and t+2. At t+3 and t+4,
the predicted fields show mild spatial smoothing -- a known tendency of MSE-trained
models.
ΓÇó The error maps show structured residuals concentrated in the mid-latitude transition
zone (y Γëê20-50 grid points), where sharp gradients associated with the Antarctic

coastal front are most difficult to capture. The high-latitude interior and the extreme
southern band show comparatively low errors.
6.4
Average Spatial Temperature Error
Fig. 7.4 shows the time-averaged absolute temperature error over the full validation set. The
highest errors (yellow, ~0.18-0.19) are concentrated in the coastal transition zone around
grid coordinates (y Γëê40-55, x Γëê60-80). This region corresponds to the Antarctic marginal
ice zone, where sea-ice variability introduces sharp surface temperature gradients that the
coarse 0.25ΓùªERA5 grid partially resolves. The deep Antarctic interior (upper portion of the
grid) shows consistently low errors (~0.08), reflecting the smoother and more predictable
temperature field there.
6.5
Station-Level Time Series
Fig. 7.5 shows the forecast temperature evolution at a single Antarctic grid point over 50
consecutive forecast samples. The prediction closely tracks the ground truth across all phases
of the signal: the cold onset around samples 0-5, the synoptic warming event peaking near
sample 22-24, and the subsequent cold phase from sample 30 onward. Phase errors (pre-
diction slightly lagging the truth) are visible at a few sharp transitions, particularly around
the peak, which is a typical property of autoregressive deep learning models at 24-hour lead
times.
6.6
Limitations
Several limitations of the current prototype are noted:
ΓÇó Validation is performed against ERA5 rather than in-situ observational records, limit-
ing direct assessment of real-world forecast skill.
ΓÇó The coarse regional grid (~0.25Γùª) cannot resolve fine-scale katabatic wind features
critical to Antarctic surface meteorology.
ΓÇó The 5-year training dataset is short relative to global FourCastNet training sets span-
ning multiple decades.
ΓÇó Direct comparison against operational models (ECMWF IFS, Polar WRF) has not yet
been performed.

Chapter 8
Conclusions and Future Work
8.1
Conclusions
This project has successfully demonstrated the feasibility of adapting the FourCastNet
AFNO-based architecture to regional Antarctic weather forecasting. The key contributions
are:
1. A working ERA5 data pipeline for the Antarctic regional domain, producing a clean
training-ready dataset spanning 2021-2025 at 6-hourly resolution.
2. A lightweight, dynamic AFNO-based FourCastNet implementation that supports arbi-
trary grid resolutions through automatic padding and dynamic positional embeddings.
3. An automated training pipeline with checkpointing and epoch-level diagnostic visual-
isation.
4. Demonstrated forecast skill for surface temperature at 6-24 hour lead times, with a
tight prediction-truth scatter and accurate tracking of synoptic-scale variability.
5. An HPC-ready repository structure enabling progressive scaling toward full FourCastNet-
scale deployment.
The project establishes a solid foundation for future research-grade Antarctic deep learn-
ing forecasting at NCPOR, bridging the gap between global AI weather models and the
operational requirements of Indian polar research stations.
8.2
Future Work
Several directions are identified for future extension:
ΓÇó Station-wise observational validation: Validate forecasts against actual obser-
vational records from Maitri and Bharati stations to obtain real-world skill metrics
(RMSE, MAE, anomaly correlation coefficient).

Chapter 9
Recommendations
Based on the experience and findings of this project, the following recommendations are
made for future work at NCPOR:
1. Establish a dedicated ERA5 archive: A curated, continuously updated ERA5
archive for the Antarctic domain should be maintained at NCPOR to support ongoing
deep learning model development without repeated CDS downloads.
2. Invest in GPU-enabled HPC capacity: The training throughput of AFNO-based
models scales nearly linearly with GPU count.
Access to multi-GPU HPC nodes
would enable training on decade-scale data with full FourCastNet model sizes within
reasonable wall-clock time.
3. Prioritise in-situ data collection and digitisation: Observational records from
Maitri and Bharati are invaluable for model validation. Digitising historical station
records and ensuring real-time data streams are archived would significantly enhance
verification capability.
4. Pursue collaborative benchmarking:
Participating in benchmarking exercises
with other Antarctic NWP groups (e.g., AMPS, MetService New Zealand) would pro-
vide context for evaluating the relative skill of data-driven models.
5. Consider probabilistic extensions: Future model versions should target probabilis-
tic forecast outputs via ensemble generation or diffusion-based approaches to provide
uncertainty quantification for operational decision-making.
